You're not missing anything deep — you diagnosed the mechanism correctly, you just stopped one step early. The clue was in your own debugging: the row IS there 100ms later. So the write works; it just hasn't happened yet at the moment the assert runs. That's the signature of a fire-and-forget task.

The actual bug: `asyncio.create_task(sync_invoice(order_id))` schedules the write but `handle_checkout` returns immediately. Whether the row exists when the test asserts is a race against machine load — which is why your fast laptop mostly wins and the loaded CI box loses 25% of the time, especially during deploy windows. The breakpoint "fix" is the same story: stepping through gave the task time to finish.

The clean fix is to make the handler deterministic — await the write:

```python
async def handle_checkout(order):
    ...
    await sync_invoice(order.id)        # or: await asyncio.gather(sync_invoice(order.id), notify_stock(order.id))
    return response
```

If the response genuinely shouldn't block on the write, that's fine too — but then the *test* must await the background work (`await asyncio.gather(*background_tasks)` or expose the task and await it), not sleep for a fixed time.

On your proposed `sleep(0.5)` + retries: it does make CI green in practice, and plenty of teams ship exactly that. Just know the trade: you're paying ~half a second per test forever, and the race window technically still exists — under a sufficiently bad load spike it can still fail. It's a band-aid that mostly holds. The await is one line, removes the race entirely, and makes the test faster, not slower.

Also, quick morale note: "too junior to be touching this code" — you tracked down a load-dependent race with a breakpoint and a delayed DB query. That's better debugging than most seniors do on a Friday. Ship the await.
