Three rejections in three months is genuinely demoralizing — I'm not going to talk you out of the tiredness. But I want to examine the conclusion you've drawn from it, because "the interviews went badly" and "I am broken" are two very different sentences, and only one of them is supported.

**Situation (the facts):** Three interviews in May, June, July. Three rejections. Last time, the "tell me about yourself" answer ran four minutes instead of 90 seconds. There's an interview tomorrow at 9am.

**Hot thought:** "Three in a row means something is broken in me. I choke — it's who I am now, and tomorrow's panel will see it in five minutes."

**Distortions at work:** Overgeneralization — three data points in one market, extrapolated to "never" and "always." Labeling — "I choke" turns a *behavior under stress* into an *identity*. Fortune telling — the panel's reaction is being decided in advance, before anyone has met you. And there's some disqualifying the positive underneath: "why bother rehearsing, it never changes anything" writes off every skill you actually have.

**Evidence for the hot thought:** The rejections are real. The rambling answer is real. Those hurt and they're worth naming.

**Evidence against:** Rejections are decisions about fit, headcount, timing, and other candidates as much as performance — three outcomes can't isolate one cause. You keep getting interviews, which means your résumé and experience clear every screen: panels keep wanting to meet the person your hot thought says is un-hireable. And "choking" is a stress *response*, which is exactly the kind of thing practice measurably changes — it's not a personality organ. So try this one: if a colleague told you they'd had three rejections and concluded they were broken, would you agree with their math? What would you point out to them?

**Balanced thought:** "I have a specific, trainable skill gap — talking about myself under pressure. Three rejections are data about the market and that gap, not a verdict on whether I'm worth hiring."

**Behaviorally:** here's tomorrow's experiment, and notice it doubles as a test of "rehearsing never changes anything": tonight at 8pm, say the answer below out loud three times, timed. In the morning, before you leave, once more. At the interview, run the experiment and watch: does the rehearsed version ramble the way the unrehearsed one did? That's how you find out whether "it never changes anything" is true — by testing it, not debating it.

Now your deliverable — your 60–90 second answer, built on your real background:

"I've spent six years in customer success, most recently leading our renewals team — I owned the retention strategy, the renewals process, and a team of people who collectively saved our biggest accounts during a rough year. Last year I took a career break to care for my family, which taught me more about prioritization and staying calm under real pressure than any job has. I'm now looking for a role where I can own customer relationships end to end and grow into broader leadership — which is exactly why this one caught my eye."

(Structure to remember under pressure: present → past → future. If you blank, it's okay to say "let me gather that thought for a second" — a pause reads as thoughtfulness, not failure.)

And yes — there is a point to a morning plan: out-loud run at 7:30, breakfast, arrive 15 minutes early, breathe slowly for two minutes in the lobby. Not because tomorrow is "already won," but because preparation is the one variable you control.

One question for you: what do you want the panel to remember about you when you leave the room? Build tomorrow around that sentence — what is it?
