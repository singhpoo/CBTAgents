It's 3am, you're frightened, and you deserve a straight answer — so here is the honest one, and it starts with this: I can't tell you whether you have a heart condition, and neither can Google, and neither can I reassure you that you don't. That's not evasive; it's the actual truth of what can be known from your bed at 3am. The only thing that can answer the heart question is a doctor with a stethoscope and an ECG. What I *can* do is separate what's happening from what your mind is concluding.

**Situation (what a camera would record):** Three nights of a fluttering/skipping sensation when lying down. Pulse around 92. A stressful month. It's 3am. You feel dread in your chest. You read two web pages, one of which mentioned cardiac arrest.

**Hot thought:** "I have a heart condition — the dread is my body telling me something is wrong."

**Distortions at work:** Emotional reasoning — "I feel dread, therefore the dread proves danger." Feelings are real *as feelings*, but they are not evidence of a cardiac diagnosis; fear produces exactly this sensation on its own. Catastrophizing — from "fluttering" to "sudden cardiac arrest" in two clicks, taking the worst page on the internet as your diagnosis.

**Evidence for the hot thought:** The sensations are real, they recur, and any new, recurring physical symptom deserves to be taken seriously — that part of you is right.

**Evidence against:** A pulse of 92 is within normal range. Palpitations when lying down, during a high-stress month, at night — with the pounding amplified by anxiety's adrenaline — is one of the most common and benign patterns there is. And notice the loop you're in: fear → noticing heartbeat → more fear → louder heartbeat. The dread is also *consistent with* the anxiety explanation, which is doing a lot of work against "it must be my heart." Here's the question that matters: if a friend described exactly these symptoms at 3am after a brutal month, what would you tell them the dread proves?

**Balanced thought:** "My body is giving me a signal worth checking properly — which means a doctor, soon, not a search engine at 3am. Anxiety can produce every sensation I'm feeling, and I cannot know which explanation is true until someone qualified listens to my heart."

**Behaviorally:** for tonight — sit up, put a light on, and do slow exhales: in for 4, out for 6, for two minutes (long exhales directly slow the heart via the vagus nerve, so this is not just distraction). Don't google again tonight; every search feeds the loop. Writing the symptoms down with times (three nights, lying down, ~92) turns fear into useful data for the doctor. And yes, waking your husband for company is reasonable — being alone at 3am with this makes it louder.

**Your decisions, plainly:**
- ER now? Go if you have chest pain or pressure, breathlessness at rest, fainting, or palpitations that won't stop. If none of those are present, an ER trip at 3am for a flutter is not what the situation calls for — but a GP appointment this week is non-negotiable, because new recurring palpitations deserve a real check, full stop.
- The work deadline: the appointment is one hour, early in the week if possible. Protect it like a meeting with your most important client — because in a sense, it is.
