You've spent all evening conducting a trial where you're the only suspect — let's look at the evidence, because the case against you is thinner than it feels at 2am.

**Situation (what a camera would record):** Sam came home, said little, said his day was "fine." Dinner with his phone. Skipped your usual show. Went to bed at 9:30 saying he was tired. He's been quieter than usual for about a week. That is the complete list of facts. Notice what's not on it: a single word or action directed *at* you.

**Hot thought:** "I must have done something — he's angry at me and freezing me out."

**Distortions at work:** Mind reading — you've concluded his internal state (angry, at *you*) from silence. Personalization — his quiet week must be about something you did; the possibility that his inner life has other contents (work, exhaustion, something eating at him that has nothing to do with you) has been edited out. Fortune telling — "if I let it sit it'll harden into resentment" predicts the future of a marriage from a quiet Tuesday.

**Evidence for:** The change from his baseline is real. A week of quiet is worth attention — you're not imagining that.

**Evidence against:** "Fine" and "tired" are thin data, but they're also *not* angry data. You replayed the week and your own inventory produced only guesses — questions with question marks, no scenes. And a person can be preoccupied, physically drained, or carrying something at work they don't want to bring home without a single thought about you. So, honestly: can you name one concrete thing he *said or did* this week that signals anger at you — as opposed to silence you inferred anger from? What's the boring alternative — that he's had a heavy week?

**Balanced thought:** "Sam is clearly carrying something. I don't know what it is yet, and it may have nothing to do with me. The only way to find out is to ask him — kindly, in daylight, when we're both resourced."

**Behaviorally:** here's the experiment, and it's a hard one — *do nothing tonight.* No 2am text. A tired, cornered husband at 2am will confirm your worst prediction, and a text sent while your alarm is this high will read as accusation no matter the words. The behavior that tests the "he's angry at me" theory is the opposite: a warm, curious question in daylight, where you can watch his face and hear his actual answer instead of a predicted one.

Now your deliverable — both versions, reworked from confession to curiosity:

To say in person tomorrow (better): "You've seemed far away this week. I miss you. What's going on — work, us, something else? I'm not here to guess, I'm here to listen."

If you need a text instead: "Hey — you've seemed far away this week and I miss you. Not a guess, not an accusation — just an invitation. What's going on? I'd rather hear it than imagine it."

Notice neither version apologizes for an unnamed crime — because pre-confessing teaches a marriage that silence produces apologies, and that's a lesson that gets expensive.

One question: when you replay Wednesday — the thing about his mother — is there a moment where he actually reacted, or is it a worry about something that *could* have landed badly? What did the room actually record?
