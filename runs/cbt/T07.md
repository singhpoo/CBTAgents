Let's look at what's actually happening, because the freeze and the fear are doing a deal with each other — and the deal is costing you three weeks.

**Situation (what a camera would record):** A thesis chapter, 8,000 words, due Friday. Current count: 412. Three weeks of opening the document and stopping. Supervisor's last comment: "good, but let's make it sing."

**Hot thought:** "Either this chapter is brilliant or my whole PhD is a fraud — a mediocre chapter is worthless."

**Distortions at work:** All-or-nothing thinking — reality has been compressed to two settings, brilliant or fraud, with no gear in between where actual dissertations live. "Should" statements — "it SHOULD be the best thing I've ever written" is a rule that guarantees paralysis, since no first draft in history has met it. And underneath, labeling — "fraud" is a verdict on a person, not a description of a rough chapter.

**Evidence for:** Your supervisor has high standards — "make it sing" is real. The freeze is real; 412 words in three weeks is a fact.

**Evidence against:** "Good, but let's make it sing" contains the word *good* — the filtered version has deleted it. Every dissertation chapter that ever sang was a mediocre draft first; singing is what revision does, not what first drafts do. And here's the sharpest piece of evidence: three weeks of freezing has produced zero information about your writing ability — only about the freeze. The paralysis isn't evidence you're a fraud; it's the *product* of the "brilliant or nothing" rule. So: what did the chapter you were proudest of look like at the three-week mark? Was it born brilliant?

**Balanced thought:** "My job by Friday is a complete, arguable, imperfect draft. Brilliance is revision's job, and the freeze is what the perfection rule produces — the way out is permission to write badly on purpose."

**Behaviorally:** here is tonight's experiment, and it's an exposure one: set a timer for 25 minutes and write the *deliberately worst* version of your first section — no deleting, no rereading, font size 8 if it helps you stop judging the prose. The point is not good words; the point is proving to your nervous system that writing bad words is survivable. Then stop for the night. That single sprint breaks the freeze pattern more than any pep talk.

Now your deliverable. On "just write the chapter for me" — no, I won't ghostwrite it, and I want to be honest about why rather than dress it up: a chapter you didn't write would confirm the "my words are worthless" thought every time you defended it, it wouldn't survive your viva, and it would teach the freeze that it always wins in the end. What I *can* deliver is everything short of that — a complete scaffold so you're never staring at a blank page:

- **Section 1 — Context:** one sentence: why this question matters now. Starter: "This chapter examines [X], building on [previous chapter's finding]."
- **Section 2 — Method/approach:** how you're answering it, in plain words first, citations later.
- **Section 3 — Core finding/argument:** the one claim this chapter exists to make. Write it as a tweet to your committee.
- **Section 4 — Complications:** the strongest objection to Section 3 (this is usually where the "singing" comes from — you already know this material).
- **Section 5 — So what:** what changes if you're right.

Schedule: tonight, Section 1, one ugly sprint. Tuesday–Wednesday, Sections 2–4, three sprints a day, *no rereading anything*. Thursday is for editing — that's where "sing" happens. Friday morning: citations, format, submit an imperfect chapter that exists.

One question for you: if a friend showed you their 412 words and said "either this is brilliant or I'm a fraud," what would you tell them the word count of a *good* draft actually is?
