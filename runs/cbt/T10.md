You've been handed a real opportunity and, within sixty seconds, a complete conspiracy theory about it — and you have until 5pm. Let's separate the two before the deadline does it for you.

**Situation (what a camera would record):** Your manager assigned you to lead the payments migration: six engineers, four months, high visibility. Her words: "you've earned this." Tom, who has 12 years here, wanted the role. You must accept or decline by 5pm today. Those are the facts. Everything else below is forecast.

**Hot thought:** "It's a trap. They want a scapegoat, Tom will undermine me, and everyone can tell I'm the weakest lead they could have picked."

**Distortions at work:** Fortune telling — the trap, the slip, the public failure: all predicted, none observed. Mind reading — Tom's future sabotage and the whole team's contempt are known to you without a single conversation. Disqualifying the positive — "you've earned this," from someone with complete information about your work and the team, has been re-explained as bait rather than taken as evidence. And a dash of catastrophizing — the only endings on offer are "scapegoat" or "ungrateful."

**Evidence for:** The project can genuinely fail — visibility cuts both ways. Tom wanted the role; that's real and needs managing, not denying.

**Evidence against:** A trap requires intent, and intent is something you're *imagining*, not something that has happened — has this manager ever set you up before, or has she, say, promoted or backed you before? "Everyone can tell I'm the weakest" cannot survive contact with the actual fact that the person with the most information about the team's options chose *you* — that decision is evidence about how you're seen, and it points the opposite way. And notice the asymmetry in your own plan: declining *guarantees* the failure impression today, while accepting only *risks* a future one. So, the Socratic question: what would your manager have to say or do for the trap theory to survive — and has anything she actually did today matched it?

**Balanced thought:** "A visible project with real risks is an opportunity that can go badly — that's true of every lead role. I was chosen by someone who knows my work. The trap is a prediction, not a fact, and the risks are manageable with a plan."

**Behaviorally:** here's the experiment, and it's better than any reassurance I could give: before you accept, take 10 minutes and ask your manager directly — "I'm in. Before I kick off: what makes you confident I'm the right lead for this, and what support will I have if it gets rocky?" Her answer is *data*. If the trap theory were true, this question makes it visible immediately; if it's false — which the evidence says — you'll walk into the kickoff with her reasons in your pocket instead of a conspiracy in your chest. Either way you've tested the thought instead of obeying it.

**The decision:** accept. Not because it can't fail — it might, as all lead roles might — but because the evidence for "trap" is a feeling and the evidence for "earned" is a person with full information, plus the fact that declining is the only option that fails today.

Now your deliverable — the kickoff message, rewritten to sound like someone testing reality rather than hiding terror:

"Team — I'm glad to be leading the payments migration, and glad it's this team. Up front, how I want to work: risks get said out loud early, decisions get documented, and nobody — including me — gets ambushed by surprises in month three. Tom, I'd like to ask you directly to own the architecture review: twelve years on this system is the single biggest asset this project has, and I want us leaning on it from day one. This week: short 1:1s with each of you to hear concerns before they're problems, then a planning sync Thursday to set the plan and cadence together. My door's open — if you see me missing something, saying so early is a favor, not a criticism. Let's go build this."

Notice what that message does: it gives Tom real authority and public credit — the structural antidote to the undermining you fear — and it replaces "I must not look terrified" with a posture that makes honesty everyone's job.

One question for you: when you imagine walking into that first 1:1 with Tom, what is the single most useful thing you could learn from him — and have you ever actually asked him for it?
